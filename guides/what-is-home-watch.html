What Is Home Watch? The Complete Guide for Absentee Homeowners

Home watch explained: what a home watcher checks, who needs the service, how visits and reports work, and how it differs from security monitoring.

By the Home.Watch editorial team. Updated Nov 18, 2026. 4 min read.

On this page

  1. Why an empty house needs eyes on it
  2. What a home watch visit usually includes
  3. Common add-on services
  4. Home watch versus remote monitoring
  5. Who uses home watch
  6. How to get started

Home watch is a scheduled, documented visual inspection of a house while its owner is away. A trained home watcher walks the property inside and out, looks for the problems that grow quietly in an empty home — a slow leak, a failed air conditioner, a tripped breaker, a broken window — and sends the owner a dated report, usually with photos. If something is wrong, the watcher calls the owner and, with permission, gets the right vendor out.

The service grew up in seasonal markets like Florida, Arizona and the Carolinas, where millions of homes sit empty for months each year. Today it serves anyone whose house is regularly unoccupied: second-home owners, people travelling for work, families settling an estate, sellers who have already moved, and homeowners in long-term care or recovering elsewhere.

Why an empty house needs eyes on it

An occupied home is monitored constantly without anyone noticing. You hear the drip, smell the musty air, see the puddle under the water heater and notice the ceiling stain the day it appears. When nobody is there, the same small failure can run for weeks.

  • Water is the costliest risk. A supply line that fails on day three of a four-month absence can soak floors, drywall and cabinets until mold sets in.
  • Climate control matters more than most owners expect. In humid regions a failed air conditioner lets humidity climb above 60% and mold can begin within days. In cold regions a failed furnace means frozen pipes.
  • Security problems often start small: a door left unlatched by a contractor, a broken window, a pile of packages announcing that nobody is home.
  • Insurance can be affected. Many homeowners policies limit coverage when a home is vacant or unoccupied beyond a set number of days unless someone checks on it. Read our guide to vacancy clauses.

What a home watch visit usually includes

Every provider sets its own checklist, but a thorough interior and exterior visit commonly covers:

  • Doors, windows, locks and garage doors are secure, with no signs of forced entry
  • Thermostat is working and holding the set temperature; humidity is in range
  • Running every faucet briefly and flushing toilets to keep traps full and spot leaks
  • Looking under sinks, around toilets, water heaters, washing machines and ice makers
  • Checking ceilings and walls for stains, cracks or bubbling paint
  • Smelling for gas, mold, sewage or pests and looking for droppings or insect activity
  • Confirming the refrigerator and freezer are running
  • Checking the electrical panel for tripped breakers
  • Walking the exterior: roof debris, gutters, landscaping, irrigation, pool equipment, storm damage
  • Collecting flyers, packages and door hangers that signal an empty home

After the visit, the owner receives a report. Good reports are timestamped, list every item checked, include photos of anything unusual and are delivered the same day.

Common add-on services

Many home watch businesses also offer services that are useful to absent owners:

  • Storm preparation and post-storm checks — installing shutters, bringing in furniture, photographing condition before and after a storm
  • Key holding and vendor access — meeting the cleaner, pest control, HVAC tech or contractor and locking up after them
  • Pre-arrival preparation — turning on water and air, stocking basics, airing the house out before you return
  • Vehicle starts and tire checks for cars left behind
  • Pool and spa checks between regular service visits

Home watch versus remote monitoring

Leak sensors, smart thermostats and cameras are excellent early-warning tools and we recommend them. But sensors only see what they are pointed at, rely on power and internet, and cannot open a door, shut a valve by hand or call your insurer with photos. The best setup combines both: sensors for instant alerts and a person for the walk-through. Our smart monitoring guide covers the technology side.

Who uses home watch

  • Seasonal residents ("snowbirds") who split the year between two homes
  • Owners of cottages, cabins, ski homes and beach houses
  • Professionals on long work assignments or travelling nurses and contractors
  • Adult children managing a parent's home after a move to assisted living
  • Executors and heirs holding a property during probate
  • Sellers whose house is on the market after they have relocated
  • Owners of homes under construction or renovation

Frequently asked questions

Is home watch the same as house sitting?

No. A house sitter lives in or stays at the home. A home watch professional visits on a schedule, inspects the property, reports what they find and leaves. Nobody stays overnight.

Does a home watcher make repairs?

Usually not. Home watch is a visual inspection service. When a problem turns up, the watcher reports it and, if you approve, coordinates a plumber, electrician or other vendor.

How often should my home be checked?

Many insurers and providers suggest at least every 7–14 days for an empty home. Weekly checks are common in hot, humid or freezing climates where small failures become big losses quickly.
